# Prime revelation and an actual conditional budget obstruction

All logarithms are natural. The cutoff is strict throughout: an integer
equal to the cutoff has zero tent weight and is omitted. This note proves
an exact finite-prime revelation recursion and an eventual failure of the
full square budget on a specified conditioning event. It does not decide
the unconditional sign of the arithmetic quantity $W(x)$.

## The actual prior and the complete budget

For real $x>1$, put
$$
Z_x=\sum_{1\le n<x}\frac{x-n}{\sqrt n},\qquad
P_x(n)=\frac{x-n}{\sqrt n\,Z_x}.
\tag{1}
$$
For a prime $p$, define
$$
m_p(n)=\log p\sum_{j=1}^{v_p(n)}p^{j/2},\qquad
S(n)=\sum_p m_p(n),\qquad z_x(n)=x/n-1.
\tag{2}
$$
In particular, $S(1)=0$. Only primes below $x$ can contribute, so
these are finite random variables on the support of $P_x$.

Use the exact density term
$$
R(z)=\frac1z\int_0^z(z-u)\log(1+u)\,du,
\qquad Q(z)=z^2/4-R(z),\qquad z>0.
\tag{3}
$$
Write
$$
H_x(n)=S(n)-1-z_x(n)/2,
\qquad
\mathcal D_x(n)=H_x(n)^2-\sum_p m_p(n)^2-Q(z_x(n)).
\tag{4}
$$
The complete arithmetic readout derived in
[the allocation identity](prime-allocation-critical-identity.md), equation (24), is
$$
W(x)/Z_x=\mathbb E_x\mathcal D_x.
\tag{5}
$$
The diagonal in (4) is the result of that exact regrouping; it is not a
replacement for the literal same-prime allocation diagonal. The endpoint
constant $-1$ and both continuous-density contributions remain in (4).

## Finite Euler product and the conditional law

Let $R$ be a finite set of primes. An integer is called $R$-smooth if
all its prime factors belong to $R$; this includes the integer $1$.
For every real $y$, define
$$
Z_R(y)=\sum_{\substack{1\le m<y\\m\text{ is }R\text{-smooth}}}
\frac{y-m}{\sqrt m}.
\tag{6}
$$
Thus $Z_R(y)=0$ when $y\le1$, and $Z_R(y)>0$ when $y>1$.
For $y>1$, let $P_{R,y}$ be the corresponding normalized prior.

This prior comes from an actual finite Euler product. Let
$(J_p)_{p\in R}$ be independent with
$$
\mathbb P(J_p=j)=(1-p^{-1/2})p^{-j/2},\qquad j\ge0,
$$
and put $N_R=\prod_{p\in R}p^{J_p}$. Unique factorization gives
$$
\mathbb P(N_R=m)=c_Rm^{-1/2},\qquad
c_R=\prod_{p\in R}(1-p^{-1/2}),
$$
on the $R$-smooth integers. Consequently
$$
\mathbb E(y-N_R)_+=c_RZ_R(y).
$$
Tilting this product law by $(y-N_R)_+$ gives exactly $P_{R,y}$.
The independent law is used before the tent tilt; independence is not
asserted after that tilt.

For the full prior (1), take $R_x=\{p:p<x\}$. Suppose the valuations
at a subset $E\subseteq R_x$ have been revealed as $(j_p)_{p\in E}$,
and put
$$
a=\prod_{p\in E}p^{j_p},\qquad R=R_x\setminus E,
\qquad y=x/a.
$$
This revealed event has positive probability exactly when $a<x$.
Every integer on the event has the unique form $n=am$, where $m$ is
$R$-smooth. Its unnormalized weight is
$$
\frac{x-am}{\sqrt{am}}
=\sqrt a\,\frac{y-m}{\sqrt m}.
$$
The common factor $\sqrt a$ cancels. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\quad
\mathcal L(N/a\mid(v_p(N))_{p\in E}=(j_p)_{p\in E})
=P_{R,x/a}.
\quad}
\tag{7}
$$

Now reveal $p\in R$, and put $R'=R\setminus\{p\}$. Splitting
$m=p^jb$ gives the finite identity
$$
Z_R(y)=\sum_{j\ge0}p^{j/2}Z_{R'}(y/p^j).
\tag{8}
$$
Terms with $p^j\ge y$ vanish. Hence the exact transition probabilities
and continuation laws are
$$
\boxed{\quad
\pi_j(R,y)=\frac{p^{j/2}Z_{R'}(y/p^j)}{Z_R(y)},\qquad
\mathcal L(m/p^j\mid v_p(m)=j)=P_{R',y/p^j}.
\quad}
\tag{9}
$$
Every allowed proper power is included. If $y=p^j$, the corresponding
term is zero because $Z_{R'}(1)=0$.

## The exact martingale response

Let $S_R$ denote the score in (2) restricted to primes in $R$, and
define, for $y>1$,
$$
F_R(y)=\mathbb E_{R,y}\left[
S_R(m)-\frac12(y/m-1)\right].
\tag{10}
$$
For an exponent $j$, abbreviate
$$
m_p(j)=\log p\sum_{\ell=1}^jp^{\ell/2}.
$$
Equation (9) gives
$$
\boxed{\quad
F_R(y)=\sum_{p^j<y}\pi_j(R,y)
\bigl[m_p(j)+F_{R'}(y/p^j)\bigr].
\quad}
\tag{11}
$$
For the empty prime set, the remaining integer is $1$, so
$$
Z_\varnothing(y)=y-1,\qquad
F_\varnothing(y)=-(y-1)/2\qquad(y>1).
\tag{12}
$$

Choose any ordering of the finitely many primes below $x$, and let
$\mathcal F_k$ be the sigma-algebra generated by the first $k$
revealed valuations. If the current revealed product is $a$, the
remaining set is $R$, and the revealed score is $S_E$, then (7) gives
$$
M_k:=\mathbb E_x[H_x\mid\mathcal F_k]
=S_E-1+F_R(x/a).
\tag{13}
$$
At the next revelation its increment is exactly
$$
\Delta_{k+1}
=m_p(J_p)+F_{R'}(y/p^{J_p})-F_R(y).
\tag{14}
$$
Its conditional mean is zero by (11). All variables have finite support,
so the usual tower-property proof of orthogonality requires no limiting
argument. Since the final martingale value is $H_x$,
$$
\boxed{\quad
\mathbb E_xH_x^2=(\mathbb E_xH_x)^2+
\sum_k\mathbb E_x\Delta_{k+1}^2.
\quad}
\tag{15}
$$
In particular, writing $b_j=m_p(j)+F_{R'}(y/p^j)$, the conditional
increment variance is
$$
\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(b_j)
=\frac12\sum_{j,\ell}\pi_j\pi_\ell(b_j-b_\ell)^2.
\tag{16}
$$
No sign for covariances under an arbitrary smooth subprior is assumed.

At the last prime, the varying part of (13) is
$$
m_p(j)-1-\frac12(y/p^j-1).
\tag{17}
$$
Both $m_p(j)$ and the negative density correction strictly increase
with $j$. More explicitly, with $z_j=y/p^j-1$,
$$
\operatorname{Var}(m_p-z/2)
=\operatorname{Var}(m_p)+\tfrac14\operatorname{Var}(z)
-\operatorname{Cov}(m_p,z).
\tag{18}
$$
When $y>p$, both exponents $0$ and $1$ have positive probability.
The independent-copy covariance formula shows
$\operatorname{Cov}(m_p,z)<0$, because $m_p(j)$ increases and
$z_j$ decreases. Thus this density covariance is a positive variance
cost at the terminal revelation.

## The actual one-prime conditioning event

Fix a prime $p$. For the full prior $P_y$, define
$$
E_{p,y}=\{n: v_q(n)=0\text{ for every prime }q\ne p\}
=\{p^j:p^j<y,\ j\ge0\}.
\tag{19}
$$
This is an actual event in the original finite probability space. It has
positive probability for every $y>1$, since it contains $n=1$, and
$$
P_y(E_{p,y})=Z_{\{p\}}(y)/Z_y.
\tag{20}
$$
Conditional on this event, the exponent distribution is
$$
\mathbb P_{p,y}(j)=
\frac{(y-p^j)p^{-j/2}}{Z_{\{p\}}(y)},\qquad p^j<y.
\tag{21}
$$
If $p$ is revealed last, this is exactly the branch on which every
previously revealed exponent is zero.

On (19), $S=m_p$ and $\sum_qm_q^2=m_p^2$. Expanding (4) without
dropping the endpoint or density terms gives
$$
\boxed{\quad
\mathcal D_y=1+z+R(z)-(z+2)m_p
\quad\text{on }E_{p,y}.
\quad}
\tag{22}
$$

## Asymptotic with explicit summable bounds

Put $r=p^{-1/2}$ and $c_p=\log p/(1-r)$. The exact finite geometric
sum in the score is
$$
m_p(j)=c_p(p^{j/2}-1),\qquad j\ge0.
\tag{23}
$$
For $y>1$,
$$
y-1\le Z_{\{p\}}(y)\le\frac{y}{1-r}.
\tag{24}
$$
Furthermore,
$$
\frac{Z_{\{p\}}(y)}y
=\sum_{j\ge0}r^j(1-p^j/y)_+
\longrightarrow\frac1{1-r}.
\tag{25}
$$
Dominated convergence in (25) uses the explicit summable majorant
$r^j$, whose sum is $(1-r)^{-1}$.

Direct integration in (3) gives
$$
R(z)=\frac{(1+z)^2}{2z}\log(1+z)-\frac34z-\frac12,
\qquad
R(z)\sim\frac12z\log z\quad(z\to\infty).
\tag{26}
$$
The integral formula also gives, for every $z>0$,
$$
0\le R(z)\le\min\left\{\frac{z^2}{6},
\frac z2\log(1+z)\right\}.
\tag{27}
$$
The two upper bounds follow respectively from
$\log(1+u)\le u$ and $\log(1+u)\le\log(1+z)$ on $[0,z]$.

For a fixed $j$, (21), (25), and (26) imply
$$
\mathbb P_{p,y}(j)\longrightarrow(1-r)r^j,
\qquad
\frac{R(y/p^j-1)}{y\log y}\longrightarrow\frac1{2p^j}.
\tag{28}
$$
Here and below a summand is defined to be zero when $p^j\ge y$.
For $y\ge2$, (24) gives
$\mathbb P_{p,y}(j)\le2r^j$. On the support, (27) gives
$$
R(y/p^j-1)\le\frac{y}{2p^j}\log y.
$$
Consequently
$$
0\le
\frac{\mathbb P_{p,y}(j)R(y/p^j-1)}{y\log y}
\le p^{-3j/2}.
\tag{29}
$$
The right side has sum $(1-p^{-3/2})^{-1}$, independent of $y$.
Dominated convergence over all exponents therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\quad
\frac{\mathbb E_{p,y}R(z)}{y\log y}
\longrightarrow
\frac{1-p^{-1/2}}{2(1-p^{-3/2})}.
\quad}
\tag{30}
$$

The negative score term in (22) is of smaller order, with an explicit
bound. For $j\ge1$ and $p^j<y$, its unnormalized summand is
$$
\begin{aligned}
&(y-p^j)p^{-j/2}m_p(j)(y/p^j+1)\\
&\qquad=c_p(1-p^{-j/2})(y^2/p^j-p^j)
\le c_py^2p^{-j}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The exponent $j=0$ contributes zero. Summing and using (24),
$$
\boxed{\quad
0\le\mathbb E_{p,y}[(z+2)m_p]
\le\frac{c_py^2}{(p-1)(y-1)}
\le\frac{2c_p}{p-1}y\qquad(y\ge2).
\quad}
\tag{31}
$$
Also $0<1+z=y/p^j\le y$, so
$$
0<\frac{\mathbb E_{p,y}(1+z)}{y\log y}
\le\frac1{\log y}.
\tag{32}
$$
Combining (22), (30), (31), and (32) yields
$$
\boxed{\quad
\lim_{y\to\infty}
\frac{\mathbb E_y[\mathcal D_y\mid E_{p,y}]}{y\log y}
=\frac{1-p^{-1/2}}{2(1-p^{-3/2})}>0.
\quad}
\tag{33}
$$
All limits are for a fixed prime $p$ and real $y\to\infty$.
The bounds already include cutoffs passing through exact prime powers.

An explicit, conservative cutoff is available without extracting a rate
from dominated convergence. For $y\ge e^3$, equation (26) gives
$$
R(y-1)\ge\tfrac12y\log y-\tfrac34y+\tfrac14
\ge\tfrac14y\log y.
$$
Equations (21) and (24) give
$\mathbb P_{p,y}(0)\ge(1-r)/2$ for $y\ge2$. Retaining just this
positive $R$-term and applying (31) to all negative terms proves
$$
\mathbb E_y[\mathcal D_y\mid E_{p,y}]
\ge y\left[\frac{1-r}{8}\log y-\frac{2c_p}{p-1}\right]
\qquad(y\ge e^3).
\tag{34}
$$
In particular, if
$$
y\ge Y_p:=\exp\left(3+
\frac{16\log p}{(p-1)(1-p^{-1/2})^2}\right),
$$
then
$$
\mathbb E_y[\mathcal D_y\mid E_{p,y}]
\ge\frac{3(1-p^{-1/2})}{8}y>0.
\tag{35}
$$

## What the obstruction does and does not exclude

Equations (33)--(35) concern the full budget, not just a covariance or an
atomic truncation. They show that the inequality
$$
\mathbb E_y[H_y^2\mid E_{p,y}]
\le\mathbb E_y\left[\sum_qm_q^2+Q(z_y)\mid E_{p,y}\right]
$$
fails eventually on an event of positive probability in the actual prior.
Thus a proof that attempts to establish that same full budget separately
on every revealed-history branch cannot succeed.

The event probability (20) can decrease with the cutoff. Its positive
conditional defect neither determines the unconditional expectation nor
prevents negative contributions from other histories. In particular,
the separately proved negative cross-prime covariances under the full
prior are compatible with this obstruction; no corresponding assertion
for every smooth conditional prior is used here. A successful use of
the exact martingale recursion must allow compensation between histories.
The sign of the full $W(y)$ remains unresolved by this argument.

The [conditional cross-prime counterexample](conditional-prime-dependence.md) is a different obstruction: it concerns positive covariance after a specific rough-number conditioning. The present theorem computes the complete square budget on a one-prime branch and proves its positive asymptotic. Neither statement replaces the original diagonal or the density terms by an independence assumption. The recursion and variance decomposition use the classical finite Doob martingale identities; no priority claim is made.
